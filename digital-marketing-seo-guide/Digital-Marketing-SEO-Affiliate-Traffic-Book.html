Chapter 01

The Big Picture, and Your 7-Day Roadmap

Before any tool or account, understand the shape of the whole thing: you will pick products worth promoting, build a home for your content, and then run two engines side by side — an organic engine (SEO, blogging, UGC content) that's slower to start but free and long-lasting, and an optional paid engine (Google Ads, Facebook Ads) that's faster to test but costs money per click. Below is the order to learn both, spread across seven days.

How the money actually flows

You write a blog post or make a video about a pet product → a reader clicks your specially-tagged affiliate link → they buy on Amazon (or another store) → the store pays you a small percentage as a commission, at no extra cost to the buyer. Your only job is getting the right person to that link — everything in this book is really just different ways of doing that one thing.

Chapter 02

Finding Pet Products That Actually Sell

This is the single most important chapter to get right — every blog post, every video, and every ad you ever make will be about a product you choose here. Seven simple checks, no guessing.

1. Look at what's already selling

Go to Amazon's 'Best Sellers' page for the Pet Supplies category, and also check the 'Movers & Shakers' page (products whose sales are rising fastest right now). Write down any product that keeps showing up across both lists — that repetition is a strong signal, not a coincidence.

2. Check how badly people want it

Open each product page and look at two numbers: the review count and the star rating. A product with 2,000+ reviews and a 4.2+ rating has proven, repeated demand. A product with 12 reviews might be new and risky, or might just be niche — either way, treat it as unproven until you see more evidence.

3. Check the price and the payout together

A product's price tells you two things: how much a buyer is willing to spend, and how much you'll earn per sale (your commission is usually a percentage of the price, so higher-priced items pay more per sale even at the same percentage). But don't chase price alone — a ₹150 product that sells constantly can out-earn a ₹3,000 product that sells once a year.

4. Check if it solves a repeating problem

Products tied to a recurring need (food, litter, flea treatment, grooming supplies) get bought again and again by the same person — which means your content keeps earning long after you publish it. Products tied to a one-time need (a carrier, a bed) are still worth covering, but they won't repeat the same way.

5. Check if you can show it, not just describe it

A product that looks impressive or dramatic on camera (an automatic feeder, a self-cleaning litter box, an oddly satisfying grooming tool) will always outperform a plain one in UGC-style video content, because the video itself becomes the hook. If a product is hard to make interesting on camera, it can still work for blog content, but it's a weaker pick for your video-first traffic.

6. Check what people are actually typing into Google

Type the product name into Google and see what autocompletes underneath it, and scroll to the 'People also ask' box. If you see phrases like 'is [product] worth it', '[product] vs [competitor]', or 'best [product] for [pet]', that tells you exactly what kind of blog post to write about it — this step alone will hand you your first ten post ideas.

7. Score it before you commit

Give each shortlisted product a quick score out of 5 on: demand (reviews/rating), payout (price × commission), repeat-purchase likelihood, and how camera-friendly it is. Anything that scores well on three or more of these is worth writing about first. Anything that scores well on all four goes to the very top of your list.

A simple example

Say you're considering a dog dental chew. It's on Amazon's Best Sellers list (check 1 ✓), has 8,000 reviews at 4.4 stars (check 2 ✓), costs ₹450 with a decent commission (check 3 ✓), it's something owners buy monthly, not once (check 4 ✓), and it looks satisfying on video when a dog enthusiastically chews it (check 5 ✓). That's a strong pick — write about it first.

Joining an affiliate program, step by step

1. Choose your program

Start with Amazon Associates (or your country's equivalent) because it covers almost every pet product in one place and is the easiest to get approved for. You can add specialised pet-brand affiliate programs later once your site has some content and traffic.

2. Have your site ready first

Most programs want to see a working website with a handful of pages before they approve you — an empty site with no content is the single most common reason beginners get rejected. Publish at least 3–5 pages before you apply.

3. Apply and describe your site honestly

Fill in the application with your real site URL and a short, honest description (e.g. 'A blog reviewing pet products for dog and cat owners in India'). Vague or misleading descriptions slow down approval.

4. Get your unique tracking links

Once approved, every link you generate through the program's dashboard is uniquely tagged to you — this is how the program knows to pay you when someone buys through your link. Never share a product's normal URL; always generate the tracked version.

5. Add the required disclosure

By law in most countries (and by every program's rules), you must clearly tell readers you earn a commission on purchases made through your links. A simple line at the top of each post — 'This post contains affiliate links; we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you' — is enough, and it also builds trust rather than hurting it.

Chapter 03

Building the Home for Your Content

Your blog/landing page is where every piece of content eventually points back to — it's what turns a viral video or a ranked blog post into an actual sale. Get this basic structure right once, and you won't need to touch it again for months.

The six pages every affiliate site needs

PageWhy it exists
Home pageA short, clear page that says what your site is about and points visitors to your best content or top products.
Blog / ArticlesWhere all your product reviews, comparisons, and guides live. This is your main traffic engine.
Individual post pagesOne page per product or topic (e.g. 'Best Dog Food for Puppies in India') — these are the pages that actually rank in Google.
About pageA few honest lines about who you are and why you write about pets — this builds the trust Google and readers both look for.
Contact pageA simple form or email address. Required for credibility, and some affiliate programs check for it.
Disclosure / Affiliate disclaimer pageOne page stating plainly that you earn commissions from some links. Required, and quick to write.

Setting it up, step by step

1. Pick a platform

WordPress.com or WordPress.org (with hosting) is the standard choice for content sites because it's built for blogging and SEO plugins exist for almost everything. If you want something even simpler to start, Blogger is free and fine for your very first few posts, and you can move later.

2. Pick a name and get a domain

Choose a short, memorable name related to pets (avoid trademarked brand names). Buy the '.com' or '.in' version through any domain registrar — this usually costs less than a coffee per month.

3. Choose a simple, fast theme

Pick a clean, mobile-friendly theme with minimal clutter. Speed and simplicity matter more than fancy design — a slow site loses both readers and Google ranking before either even reads your content.

4. Create your six core pages

Set up the pages listed in the table above before writing your first blog post. This takes under an hour and makes your site look established rather than empty.

5. Install an SEO plugin (if using WordPress)

A free plugin like Yoast SEO or Rank Math adds a simple checklist under every post you write, telling you in plain language what to fix (e.g. 'add your keyword to the title'). This removes most of the guesswork from SEO.

6. Connect Google Search Console

This is a free tool from Google that shows you which of your pages are indexed (found and stored by Google) and what people are searching to find you. Go to search.google.com/search-console, add your site, verify ownership using the method your platform recommends (usually pasting a small code snippet or uploading a file), and submit your sitemap (a list of your pages, usually found at yoursite.com/sitemap.xml).

7. Connect Google Analytics

This free tool shows you how many people visit, which pages they read, and where they came from. Create an account at analytics.google.com, get your tracking code, and add it to your site (most themes/plugins have a simple box to paste this into — no coding needed).

Chapter 04

SEO — Getting Free Traffic From Google

SEO (Search Engine Optimisation) sounds technical, but it's really just doing three simple things well and consistently. This is the chapter that turns your blog into a source of traffic that keeps arriving long after you've stopped actively promoting a post.

How Google actually decides rankings

Google's job is to hand searchers the best possible answer, as fast as possible. It decides 'best' using three things, in this order of importance: (1) Does this page actually answer the question being searched? (2) Can Google trust this page and this site — is it well-organised, fast, and does other content on the web point to it? (3) Is the experience good — is it easy to read on a phone, free of intrusive pop-ups, and not painfully slow? Nearly everything called 'SEO' is just deliberately doing these three things well instead of by accident.

The three pillars of SEO

PillarIn plain words
On-page SEOEverything on the page itself: does it clearly answer the search, is the important information easy to find, is it well-organised with headings, and does it use the words people actually search for?
Technical SEOEverything about how the site works behind the scenes: does it load fast, does it work on mobile, can Google's crawler (the automated program that reads and files every page on the internet) actually find and read your pages without errors?
Off-page SEOEverything that happens outside your site that builds trust: other websites linking to yours (called backlinks), people sharing your content, and your site being mentioned around the web.

Keyword research, step by step (free, no tools required)

1. Start with the product name

Type the plain product or topic name into Google (e.g. 'dog dental chews') and note everything that autocompletes below it — these are real phrases people search.

2. Read the 'People also ask' box

Every Google results page has a box of related questions. These are gold: each one is a proven question real people want answered, and each can become its own section or even its own blog post.

3. Scroll to 'related searches'

At the bottom of the results page, Google lists related phrases. Add any relevant ones to your list.

4. Sort your list by intent

Group your phrases into three buckets: 'just curious' (e.g. 'why do dogs chew'), 'comparing options' (e.g. 'best dog dental chews'), and 'ready to buy' (e.g. '[product name] review' or '[product] price India'). The 'ready to buy' bucket converts best into affiliate sales — prioritise it, but don't ignore the other two, since they bring in the traffic that later becomes buyers.

5. Check the competition (in plain terms)

Search your main phrase and look at what's already ranking. If the first page is full of huge, well-known sites, that phrase will be slow to rank for as a new site. If you see smaller blogs, forums, or thin content ranking, that's a sign a new, well-written post has a real chance.

6. Pick one main phrase per post

Every blog post should be built around one main phrase (your 'target keyword') plus a handful of closely related ones. Trying to rank one post for too many unrelated phrases usually means it ranks for none of them well.

On-page SEO checklist — run this on every post before you publish

  • Your main keyword appears in the page title, naturally — not stuffed in repeatedly.
  • The title is written for a human first: it should make someone want to click, not just contain the keyword.
  • The first 2–3 sentences answer the search directly — don't make readers scroll to find out if this page is useful.
  • You use clear subheadings (Google calls these H2s and H3s) to break the post into scannable sections.
  • Every image has a short, honest description added in the 'alt text' field — this helps both accessibility and Google.
  • You link to 2–3 other relevant posts on your own site (called internal linking) — this helps Google understand your site's structure.
  • The page loads in under 3 seconds — check with Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool.
  • The post is genuinely longer and more useful than what's currently ranking — not padded, just more complete.
  • There's a clear next step at the end — a link to buy, a related post, or a question inviting comments.

Getting backlinks (off-page SEO) as a brand-new site

Answer questions where pet owners already gather

Find relevant threads on Reddit, Quora, and Facebook groups where people ask about pet products, and give a genuinely useful answer, linking to your post only when it truly adds value. This builds early traffic and, over time, some of these links get picked up and count toward your site's trust.

Get listed in relevant directories

Pet blog directories, local business directories (if applicable), and general blog directories are an easy, beginner-friendly way to get your first few backlinks.

Write a genuinely useful post other bloggers want to reference

A well-researched comparison, an original photo set, or a simple downloadable checklist gets naturally linked to by other creators far more often than a generic post.

Reach out for guest posts, once you have proof of quality

After you have 10–15 solid posts, politely email smaller pet-related blogs or local businesses offering to write a free guest post with one link back to your site. Start small and be genuinely helpful, not spammy.

Share every post on Pinterest

Pinterest behaves more like a search engine than a social feed, and pins can drive steady traffic to blog posts for years — it's one of the highest-return, lowest-effort channels for a content/affiliate site.

Chapter 05

Writing a Blog Post That Ranks and Sells

One repeatable structure, used for every post. It satisfies what Google is looking for and what a real reader who's about to spend money wants to see — in that order, because the second follows naturally from the first.

The 8-part post template

1. The hook (first 2 sentences)

Open with the exact problem or question the reader searched for. Don't warm up slowly — answer or address it immediately.

2. Quick answer / summary

For 'best X' or review-style posts, give a short, honest verdict near the top. Readers (and Google) reward pages that respect their time.

3. The main content

The detailed comparison, review, or explanation — broken into clear subheadings, with real specifics (price, size, what it's good/bad for) rather than vague praise.

4. Real photos or your own video

Even a couple of your own phone photos of the product (if you have it) or an embedded clip beats stock images — it signals to both readers and Google that the content is genuine, not copied.

5. A simple comparison table

For 'best X' posts, a short table (product, price range, best for, rating) lets skimmers decide fast — this is one of the highest-converting elements on an affiliate post.

6. Your honest recommendation

State clearly which option you'd personally pick and for whom — this is what separates a trusted review from a generic listicle.

7. FAQ section

Answer 3–4 of the exact questions from the 'People also ask' box you found during keyword research — this is an easy way to capture extra search traffic within the same post.

8. The affiliate link and disclosure

Place your tracked links naturally within the text (not just at the bottom), and keep your disclosure line visible near the top of the post.

Post types that reliably convert into affiliate sales

Post typeWhy it works
“Best [product] for [audience]”e.g. 'Best Dog Food for Puppies in India' — high buying intent, works as a listicle with a comparison table.
“[Product] Review”A deep, honest single-product review — works especially well if you actually own or have tried the product.
“[Product A] vs [Product B]”Comparison posts capture people who are already deciding between two options and are close to buying.
“Is [product] worth it?”Directly answers a doubt — very high conversion because the reader is already convinced they need something like it.
“How to [solve a pet problem]”A helpful guide (e.g. 'How to stop a dog from chewing furniture') that naturally recommends 2–3 products as the solution.
Seasonal / gift guidese.g. 'Best Pet Gifts for Diwali' — timed posts that spike predictably around known dates.
Chapter 06

UGC-Style Content — Video That Doesn't Feel Like an Ad

You don't need a camera, a studio, or editing skills you don't already have. You need a phone, good light, and a planned hook — everything else here is a repeatable checklist.

What “UGC” actually means

UGC stands for 'User-Generated Content' — it originally meant real customers filming themselves using a product, but today it also describes any video that has that same honest, unpolished, filmed-on-a-phone feel, even when a creator makes it deliberately for a brand or for affiliate content. The reason it performs so well is simple: it doesn't look like an advertisement, so people don't scroll past it out of habit the way they do with polished ads.

Filming and posting, step by step

1. Pick one product per shoot

Don't try to cover multiple products in one video — one clear product, one clear message, performs far better.

2. Write your hook before you film anything

The first 1–2 seconds decide whether someone keeps watching. Write 2–3 hook lines from Chapter 10 and pick the strongest before you press record — filming without a planned hook wastes the shoot.

3. Film in good, natural light

Shoot near a window during the day, or in bright indoor light. You don't need special equipment — a clean, well-lit phone video consistently outperforms a dim, professional-looking one on social platforms.

4. Show the problem, then the product solving it

e.g. show the mess before a grooming tool, then the result after. This 'before/after' structure is one of the most reliable formats in all of content marketing, not just pet content.

5. Keep it short and vertical

Film vertically (phone held upright) for Reels, Shorts, and TikTok. Aim for 15–30 seconds for most product content — long enough to show the payoff, short enough that people watch to the end (which the platforms reward).

6. Add simple captions

Use a free app like CapCut to add on-screen text repeating your key point — many people watch with sound off, and captions also help the algorithm understand and categorise your content.

7. End with a clear next step

e.g. 'Link in bio' or 'Full review on the blog — link above'. Always give the viewer one obvious action.

8. Post, then reuse everywhere

One filmed clip becomes: an Instagram Reel, a YouTube Short, a Pinterest video Pin, and 3–4 photo frames pulled from the video for a Pinterest image Pin or Instagram carousel. One shoot, many posts — this is what makes a content schedule sustainable.

One shoot, many posts — the repurposing map

You film/write onceIt becomes
One 30-second phone videoBecomes your Instagram Reel and YouTube Short (same file, just re-uploaded to each app).
The same video, cut into 3 still framesBecomes a Pinterest image Pin or an Instagram carousel post.

Chapter 07

Google Ads, Start to Finish

Google Ads shows your ad to people already typing a search — the highest-intent traffic that exists, because they're already looking for something like what you have. Treat this chapter as optional until your organic content (Chapters 4–6) has proven a product and a hook actually work.

Terms you'll see, explained simply

TermWhat it means
CampaignThe top-level container for a goal, e.g. 'Sell Dog Food'. Everything below it shares one budget and one objective.
Ad groupA folder inside a campaign that groups closely related keywords and ads together, e.g. 'Puppy Food' inside 'Sell Dog Food'.
KeywordA word or phrase you tell Google to show your ad for, e.g. 'best puppy food'.
Match typeA setting that controls how closely someone's search has to match your keyword before your ad shows.
BidHow much you're willing to pay when someone clicks your ad. Google usually charges slightly less than your bid, based on an auction.
Quality ScoreGoogle's rating (1–10) of how relevant and useful your ad and landing page are for a keyword — a higher score means you pay less for the same position.

Match types — how strict Google is about matching your keyword

Match typeWritten asWhat it does
Broad matchjust the keyword, no symbolsShows for loosely related searches too. Widest reach, least control — risky for beginners.
Phrase match"in quotes"Shows when the core phrase appears, in order, even with extra words around it. A good beginner default.
Exact match[in brackets]Shows only for that exact phrase or very close variants. Most control, smallest reach — good once you know what converts.

Creating your first campaign, step by step

1. Create your account

Go to ads.google.com and sign up with a Google account. Choose 'Switch to Expert Mode' if offered a simplified 'Smart' setup — Expert Mode gives you the control this book assumes.

2. Set up billing

Add a payment method under Billing. You won't be charged until your ad actually gets clicks.

3. Create a new campaign

Click 'New Campaign', choose the goal 'Sales' or 'Website traffic', then choose the campaign type 'Search' — this shows text ads to people actively typing search queries, which fits an affiliate site best as a first campaign type.

4. Set your daily budget

Start small — an amount you're fully comfortable losing while testing, e.g. ₹300–500/day. You can raise this later once you see real results.

5. Choose your location and language

Target the country/region your audience actually buys from, and the language they search in.

6. Build one tight ad group

Create an ad group around a single, specific theme (e.g. 'puppy dental chews'), not a broad one — tighter groups get higher Quality Scores and cheaper clicks.

7. Add 10–20 closely related keywords

Use Phrase match to start. Include the buying-intent phrases you found in Chapter 4's keyword research (e.g. 'best puppy dental chews', 'puppy dental chews review').

8. Add negative keywords

Negative keywords are words you tell Google NOT to show your ad for. e.g. if you only cover paid products, add 'free' as a negative keyword so you don't pay for clicks from people looking for free content.

9. Write your ad

Write 3+ headlines (30 characters each) and 2+ descriptions (90 characters each). Lead with the benefit or the exact phrase someone searched, and include a clear reason to click now.

10. Point it to the right page

Send the click to your specific blog post/review page — never your homepage. The page must clearly match what the ad promised, or people bounce immediately.

11. Review and launch

Double-check budget, location, and keywords, then publish. Google usually takes a few hours to approve a new ad.

Managing it afterwards

Check the Search Terms report weekly

This shows the exact phrases people typed that triggered your ad. Add irrelevant ones as negative keywords — this single habit saves more wasted budget than anything else.

Watch Click-Through Rate (CTR)

The percentage of people who saw your ad and clicked it. Below 2% on Search campaigns usually means your ad text needs work.

Watch Cost Per Click (CPC) and Cost Per Conversion

Know roughly what a sale is worth to you (your commission), and don't let your cost per conversion creep past that.

Pause, don't panic

If a keyword or ad is clearly underperforming after a fair test (a few hundred impressions), pause it rather than deleting — you can always learn from what didn't work.

Test one change at a time

Change either the headline, or the keywords, or the budget — not all three together — so you know what actually caused a result to change.

Chapter 08

Facebook & Instagram Ads, Start to Finish

Meta Ads (Facebook and Instagram) shows your ad to people who weren't searching for anything — you interrupt their feed. This works best with the same UGC-style video from Chapter 6, because it blends into the feed instead of looking like an ad.

Terms you'll see, explained simply

TermWhat it means
Meta Business Suite / Business ManagerThe free control panel where you manage your Facebook Page, Instagram account, ad account, and Pixel all in one place.
Pixel / Conversions APIA small tracking snippet added to your site that tells Facebook when someone who saw your ad later visited your site or bought something — without it, Facebook is flying blind on results.
Campaign → Ad Set → AdThe same nested structure as Google Ads: Campaign holds the objective, Ad Set holds the audience/budget, Ad holds the actual creative and copy.
AudienceThe group of people your ad is shown to, built from location, age, interests, or people who've already interacted with you.
Lookalike audienceA new audience Facebook builds automatically by finding people who resemble your existing customers/visitors — very powerful once you have some data to build it from.

Creating your first campaign, step by step

1. Create a Facebook Page

Every ad needs to run through a Facebook Page (even if your main presence is Instagram). Create one for your site/brand if you haven't already.

2. Set up Meta Business Suite

Go to business.facebook.com, create a Business Portfolio, and connect your Facebook Page and Instagram account to it.

3. Install the Meta Pixel on your site

In Events Manager, create a Pixel, then paste the provided code snippet into your site (WordPress plugins like 'PixelYourSite' do this without any coding).

4. Add a payment method

Set this up in Business Settings under Payments before creating your first campaign.

5. Create a new campaign

Choose the objective 'Traffic' or 'Sales' for a beginner affiliate campaign — this tells Facebook what kind of result to optimise for.

6. Set your ad set: audience

Start with a Core Audience: pick a location, an age range that matches real pet owners, and 2–3 relevant interests (e.g. 'Pet care', 'Dog', specific pet brands). Keep it broad-ish at first — Facebook's system often finds good matches on its own when given room.

7. Set your ad set: budget and schedule

Start with a small daily budget (₹300–500/day) and let it run at least 3–4 days before judging results — Facebook's delivery system needs a short 'learning period' to find the right people.

8. Choose placements

Start with 'Advantage+ placements' (automatic) so Facebook can show your ad across Feed, Reels, and Stories wherever it performs best, rather than you guessing.

9. Build the ad itself

Upload your UGC-style video or photo (Chapter 6), write a short hook as the primary text, and a clear headline. Native-feeling video consistently outperforms polished graphics here.

10. Add your link and a clear call-to-action

Point to your specific blog post/review page, and choose a button like 'Learn More' or 'Shop Now'.

11. Review and publish

Facebook typically reviews new ads within a few hours. Once approved, it starts delivering automatically.

Managing it afterwards

Give it a fair learning window

Avoid judging or editing an ad in its first 24–48 hours — constant edits reset its learning progress and waste budget.

Watch Cost Per Result and CTR

In Ads Manager, these two numbers tell you almost everything about whether an ad is working.

Test 2–3 creatives per audience

Run a couple of different videos/hooks against the same audience to see which one actually performs, then keep only the winner.

Build a retargeting ad set once you have traffic

Retargeting means showing a follow-up ad specifically to people who already visited your site but didn't buy — it's usually the cheapest, highest-converting audience once your Pixel has enough data.

Chapter 09

Turning Visitors Into Leads

Most people won't buy on their first visit. Capturing a lead means you get a second, third, and fourth chance instead of losing that visitor the moment they close the tab.

What a “lead” means here

A 'lead' is simply a visitor who gives you a way to contact them again — usually an email address or a WhatsApp number — before they've necessarily bought anything. For an affiliate site, leads matter because most visitors won't buy on their very first visit; capturing a lead means you can follow up later instead of losing that visitor forever the moment they close the tab.

Setting up lead capture, step by step

1. Create a lead magnet

A small, genuinely useful free item people trade their email for — e.g. a 'Free Puppy Care Checklist PDF' or 'Top 10 Pet Product Picks' guide. It should be quick to make and directly related to your niche.

2. Design a simple opt-in form

Use a free tool like Mailchimp (free up to a few hundred subscribers) or a simple embedded Google Form. Ask only for a name and email — every extra field lowers how many people finish signing up.

3. Place the form where attention already is

Add it near the top of your best-performing blog posts, as a simple pop-up after a few seconds on page, and at the end of every post.

4. Deliver the freebie automatically

Set up an autoresponder (an automatic email sent the moment someone signs up) inside your email tool so the lead magnet is delivered instantly, with no manual work from you.

5. Write a short welcome sequence

3–5 short, helpful emails sent over the following days/weeks — not sales pitches, but useful tips that naturally mention a product or two with your affiliate link.

6. Keep emailing regularly, briefly

A short weekly or biweekly email with one useful tip and one product mention keeps your list warm without feeling spammy.

Chapter 10

Hooks & Copywriting — The Skill Behind Everything

A “hook” is just the first line — of a blog title, a video, an ad, an email — whose only job is to stop someone from scrolling past. Everything above only works if the hook earns the next few seconds of attention.

Seven hook formulas, with ready examples

Curiosity gap

  • “Nobody tells new dog owners this one thing about food labels...”
  • “I didn't believe this litter box actually worked, until...”

Problem → Agitate → Solve

  • “Your dog's breath isn't 'just a dog thing' — here's what's actually causing it, and the ₹300 fix.”
  • “If your cat's litter box smells within a day, you're using the wrong litter — not too little.”

Before / After

  • “This is my dog's coat after 2 weeks of this supplement. No filter.”
  • “Watch what happens 10 seconds after this feeder opens.”

Social proof

  • “This is the #1 best-selling dog chew on Amazon right now — here's if it's worth it.”
  • “30,000 pet owners bought this last month. I tested it so you don't have to.”

Question hook

  • “Is your puppy's food actually good, or just well-marketed?”
  • “Would your cat pass this 5-second litter test?”

Number / list hook

  • “5 pet products that look expensive but aren't worth it.”
  • “3 signs your dog food is actually low quality.”

Urgency / timely

  • “Before you buy dog food again, watch this.”
  • “Don't order this until you've read the ingredient list with me.”

Three copywriting frameworks for longer copy

FrameworkHow it works
AIDAAttention → Interest → Desire → Action. Grab attention with a hook, build interest with a fact or story, create desire by showing the benefit/result, end with one clear action (a link or button).
PASProblem → Agitate → Solve. State the problem plainly, make the reader feel exactly how annoying/costly it is, then present the product as the clear solution.
4 PsPicture → Promise → Prove → Push. Paint a picture of the outcome, promise the benefit, prove it (reviews, your own results, data), push for the action.

Five rules that matter more than any formula

  • The hook has to be true — an exaggerated hook gets clicks once and destroys trust for every post after.
  • Put the hook in the first line of everything: blog title, video's first second, ad headline, email subject.
  • One hook, one idea — don't try to hook with two different angles in the same line.
  • Specific numbers and specific words beat vague ones ('37% less shedding' beats 'less shedding').
  • Read it out loud — if it sounds like something you'd actually say to a friend, it will work better than something that sounds like an advert.

Chapter 12

Common Mistakes, and a Plain-English Glossary

Nine mistakes that cost beginners the most time and money, followed by a complete glossary of every term used in this book, explained simply, so you never have to guess what a word means.

01Publishing an empty-looking site and applying for affiliate programs immediately

Programs reject thin sites. Publish 3–5 real posts first.

02Writing about products you'd never actually recommend

Readers and Google both reward genuine, specific opinions over generic praise — and trust, once lost, is very hard to rebuild.

03Chasing every product instead of the ones that score well

Use the Chapter 2 scoring method — a smaller list of well-chosen products beats a huge list of random ones.

04Skipping keyword research and just writing what feels interesting

A brilliant post that nobody is searching for gets no organic traffic, no matter how well it's written.

05Turning ad budget up before testing small

Always prove a small budget works before increasing it — both Google and Facebook punish sudden big jumps with unstable results.

06Editing a live ad every few hours

Both platforms need a short learning period; constant changes reset progress and waste money.

07Forgetting the affiliate disclosure

It's required, it's quick to add, and skipping it risks your account and your readers' trust.

08Filming content that looks like an advert

The entire value of UGC-style content is that it doesn't look like one — polish it just enough to be clear, not so much that it feels like a commercial.

09Giving up after one week with no sales

SEO and content compound — most sites see real traction between month 2 and month 4, not week 1. Paid ads can produce faster signal, but even those need a few honest test cycles.

In closing

You don't need every chapter mastered before you start — you need Chapter 2 (products) and Chapter 3 (your site) done, then Chapters 4–6 (SEO, blogging, UGC content) running every week, because that's the organic engine that compounds and eventually becomes free, ongoing traffic. Treat Chapters 7–9 (Google Ads, Facebook Ads, lead generation) as tools you bring in once your organic content proves which products and hooks actually work — ads spend money to find out fast what SEO would eventually tell you for free, so the two work best together, not as a replacement for each other.
